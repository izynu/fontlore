# Manual Letterform Analysis

What this reference covers: How to read letterforms yourself when tools fail or the sample is stylized: terminals, apertures, joints, and the diagnostic letters (g, a, r, y and friends) with classic named examples. This is the skill that makes every other identification method better.

Automated tools match shapes; humans read them. When the sample is distorted, rare, or drawn, your eye plus vocabulary is the best instrument available, and the vocabulary is learnable in an afternoon.

## Terminals

- Ball terminals: round dots ending strokes, common in serifs and some sans faces. Cooper Black's swelling ball terminals are the textbook example; Bodoni-style faces end many strokes in balls.
- Beak terminals: pointed, slightly flared endings like a bird's beak, characteristic of Times New Roman and many transitional serifs on letters like a, c, f, and r.
- Sheared terminals: cut flat at an angle, common in calligraphic and humanist designs; they signal a pen-derived logic.
- Flagged terminals: a small flag-like flare, seen on some grotesques' r and a.
- Terminal type is one of the fastest discriminators between superficially similar serifs; train on it first.

## Apertures and stress

- Aperture: how open the counter is. Futura's c closes almost completely; Verdana's c stays open wide even at small sizes.
- Closed apertures suggest geometric or display DNA; open apertures suggest humanist or screen-oriented design.
- Stress: the axis of thinnest strokes. Diagonal stress points to old-style calligraphic roots; vertical stress points to transitional, didone, and most modern designs.
- Contrast: extreme thick-thin (didones) versus near-monoline (many geometrics) is a first-order classification you can make across a room.

## The diagnostic letters

- g: single-story (Futura, Century Gothic) versus double-story or binocular (Times, Georgia, most text serifs). Franklin Gothic is a famous sans with a double-story g, a rare and identifying trait.
- a: single-story versus double-story; geometric sans faces split on this, and it separates lookalikes quickly.
- r: the arm's shape and terminal (ball, flag, sheared) is highly individual; compare r across candidates early.
- y: straight tail versus curved tail versus curled tail; many neo-grotesques have straight tails while humanist faces curve.
- R: straight leg, kicked leg, or curved leg; the R leg is a classic foundry signature.
- G: with spur, without spur, with a beard; the G separates Helvetica-likes from Akzidenz-likes for attentive eyes.
- Q: the tail's shape and attachment are wildly variable and often visible in logos and display settings.
- t: flat top versus angled top versus slab; and J: hooks versus straight descenders.
- Numerals: the 2's base, the 1's flag and foot, the 4's closure, and old-style versus lining figures are all diagnostic.

## Joints and construction

- Where strokes meet (the joint of b, g, p) reveals design logic: pinched joints signal contrast-driven construction; open joints signal sturdier text designs.
- Ink traps, functional or decorative, mark specific foundries and eras; large expressive traps are a feature of several contemporary designs.
- Serif brackets (the curve into the serif) versus abrupt slabs separate old-style from mechanistic constructions.
- Overall construction logic (broad-nib pen, pointed pen, geometric compass, modular system) predicts a family's neighbors.

## Building the skill

- Pick five fonts you know well and write their diagnostic profiles: terminal types, g and a forms, aperture width, stress, joint behavior.
- Compare near-identical pairs deliberately (Helvetica versus Arial versus Roboto) and find three real differences each; this is eye-training with instant feedback.
- Keep a personal notebook of distinctive letterforms seen in the wild; pattern memory compounds.
- Use the Identifont question vocabulary as your checklist; it encodes the features that actually discriminate.

## Workflow

1. From the sample, extract the clearest large letters; note lowercase and caps separately.
2. Fill in the checklist: terminals, aperture, stress, contrast, g, a, r, y, R, G, Q, t, numerals.
3. Match the profile against fonts you know, then against foundry catalogs and classification expectations.
4. Form two or three candidate hypotheses and verify each against full specimens.
5. Record the letterform evidence with the identification; profiles make reviews defensible.

## Common mistakes

- Judging by overall impression or "vibe" instead of named features; impressions are invisible in notes.
- Comparing a distorted sample against clean specimens without accounting for the distortion.
- Overweighting famous features (Helvetica's R) that several families share.
- Ignoring numerals and punctuation, which are often the only untouched glyphs in stylized settings.
- Treating the analysis as done without cross-checking against specimens at matched size.

## Decision rules

- Sample is clean and common: tools first, analysis second; do not romanticize manual work.
- Sample is stylized, distorted, or rare: analysis first, tools to confirm.
- Two hypotheses survive analysis: the difference will live in two or three specific letters; find them.
- If you cannot name a single concrete letterform feature distinguishing your top two candidates, you have not finished the analysis.

## Comparison table

The highest-yield diagnostic letters, their variants, and named examples:

| Letter | Variants to check | Classic examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| g | Single-story versus double-story; open versus closed lower loop | Futura single-story; Georgia and Roboto double-story; Inter single-story by default |
| a | Single-story versus double-story; tail, aperture, bowl join | Futura and Century Gothic single-story; most text serifs double-story |
| R | Straight, kicked, or curved leg; where the leg joins the bowl | Helvetica Neue straight diagonal; Arial curved and kicked |
| t | Top cut flat, angled, or slanted; bottom hook length | Arial's angled top cut against Helvetica's flat cut |
| y | Straight, curved, or curled tail | Straight tails common in neo-grotesques; humanist faces curve |
| G | Spur or no spur; beard; crossbar depth | Helvetica Neue carries a spur; Akzidenz-Grotesk does not |
| Q | Tail shape, attachment, inside or outside the bowl | Wildly variable; often the only expressive glyph in a lockup |
| 0 versus O | Zero slashed, dotted, narrower, or a plain oval | DIN-style faces keep a plain unslashed zero; many UI faces slash or dot it |
| l, I, 1 | Bare stem, crossbars, flag and base | Fonts split freely: some add a tail to the l for legibility, some leave l and I identical |
| r | Arm length, terminal type, curvature | Cooper Black's swelling ball terminal; Times New Roman's beak |
| e | Crossbar levelness, terminal cut angle, aperture | Humanist faces keep an angled, open e; geometric faces close it |
| s | Spine curvature, terminal cuts, contrast distribution | The s's spine is hard to fake and easy to compare |

Any two or three of these letters usually separate the top candidates in a tool's ranked list. When they do not, the candidates are probably versions or relatives of the same family, and the hunt moves to foundry catalogs and release notes rather than more squinting.

Weight changes letterforms, so run the table against the matching weight: a bold's R leg and a light's aperture can differ within one family. If the sample's weight is unknown, compare candidates in two weights before eliminating anything.

Rare glyphs are fingerprints: when the sample happens to include an ampersand, a percent sign, or a parenthesized date, analyze them first. Unfamiliar shapes carry more information than over-learned letters, and stylization tends to leave them untouched.

## Worked example

A screenshot of an intranet dashboard header, sample text large and clean, tools narrowed the field to Helvetica Neue and Arial. The analysis that decides it:

- R: the leg starts from the bowl and kicks out with a visible curve; Helvetica Neue's leg is a straight diagonal. Point one to Arial.
- G: no spur where the crossbar meets the arc; Helvetica Neue carries a clear spur. Point two to Arial.
- t: the top of the ascender is cut at an angle; Helvetica Neue's t is cut flat. Point three to Arial.
- C and S terminals: cut at an angle rather than horizontally, matching Arial's practice; a corroboration, not a decision.
- Verdict: Arial, with three independent diagnostics agreeing and a fourth corroborating.

The lesson is the shape of the evidence, not the brand name. No single letter decides alone: Arial's curved R leg appears in other grotesques, and Helvetica Neue's spurless lookalikes exist elsewhere too. The stack of agreeing diagnostics is the evidence, and the record lists them as R, G, t, and terminals, each stated in one checkable sentence.

The same method in reverse, a serif case: a book cover sample splits Garamond revivals from a Caslon by numerals and stress. The sample's old-style figures with a crossed 4 and its oblique stress point to the Garamond class; the Caslon candidates show lining figures and a more vertical axis. Again, the diff between two candidate profiles, not an impression, does the deciding.

The record this produces writes itself: each diagnostic gets a one-sentence verdict, the eliminations name the letter that eliminated them, and the confidence tier follows from how many independent diagnostics agree. Reviewers can re-run the diff in minutes, and disagreements localize to a specific letter instead of a taste dispute.

## Field notes

What a working analysis habit looks like in practice:

- Extract the clearest large letters first and transcribe exactly what is present; half the wasted analysis time comes from assuming the sample contains a g that it does not.
- Note the lowercase and caps separately; some artifacts use drawn or modified caps with untouched lowercase, and the untouched half is where identification lives.
- When the sample is a photo of signage, light angle fakes contrast and stress; check the same letter twice at different distances from the glare.
- Numerals and punctuation survive stylization better than letters; a logo that mangles its letters often leaves the ampersand and figures untouched and diagnostic.
- Keep a running list of the pairs you confuse (Helvetica Neue and Roboto at small sizes, Merriweather and PT Serif in screenshots) and add the discriminating feature each time you resolve one.
- Describe what you see in construction vocabulary (terminals, apertures, joints, stress) rather than adjectives; adjectives do not survive review, vocabulary does.
- When two hypotheses survive the full table, find the two or three letters where they differ and hunt for those letters in the sample; if the sample lacks them, say so and keep the confidence tier down.
- Time pressure changes order, not rigor: under deadline, run three diagnostics well (g, a, R) rather than ten badly, and say in the record which ones you ran.
- Stylized display settings hide construction but rarely change contrast and stress; classify first by those two, then hunt the surviving details.
- When the sample is all caps, look for the diagnostics that caps still carry: G's spur, R's leg, Q's tail, the numerals, and the punctuation.
- Two eyes beat one: have a colleague fill the same table blind and compare; the disagreements between your tables are the letters worth studying.

## Quick reference

The ten questions to ask of any sample, in order of yield:

1. g: single-story or double-story? Any unusual ear or lower loop?
2. a: single- or double-story; how open is the aperture?
3. R: straight, kicked, or curved leg, and where does it join?
4. t: how is the top cut, and how long is the bottom hook?
5. G: spur, no spur, or beard?
6. y: straight, curved, or curled tail?
7. Q: what does the tail do, and where does it attach?
8. r: what ends the arm, ball, flag, or sheared cut?
9. 0 versus O: slashed, dotted, narrower, or the same width?
10. l, I, 1: how does the face keep them apart?

Answer all ten for the sample, then for each candidate, and diff. The diff is the identification: whatever survives the diff is your evidence, stated in letters rather than adjectives, and it slots directly into the analysis field of the record.

Bonus questions when the letters survive: the ampersand's construction, the f's hook depth, the percent sign's geometry, and the quote marks' shape. Anything the sample offers beyond plain letters is welcome, because decoration draws attention and fingerprints hide in the quiet glyphs.
